Restaurants run on thin margins. According to the National Restaurant Association's 2025 operations data, the median full-service restaurant kept 2.8% of sales as pre-tax income, and the median limited-service restaurant kept 4.0%. On $1 million in sales, that is roughly $28,000 to $40,000.

That is why small gains matter so much. A few more guests a day, a slightly higher check, or one more visit a year from your regulars can add more to profit than most cost cuts.

Restaurant Sales in 2026: What the Data Says

The industry keeps growing in dollars, but most of that growth comes from higher prices. The National Restaurant Association's 2026 State of the Restaurant Industry report projects $1.55 trillion in restaurant and foodservice sales this year, up 4.8% from 2025. Adjusted for menu price inflation, real growth is closer to 1.3%.

Traffic is the harder part. The same report found that more than six in ten operators saw customer traffic decline in 2025 compared with 2024. With fewer guests to go around, the restaurants that grow are the ones that win a bigger share of each guest's visits and spending.

Guests also research more before they choose. In BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, 97% of consumers said they read online reviews for local businesses, and 31% said they only use businesses rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17% a year earlier. Your ratings now decide whether many guests consider you at all.

Here is what this means for your sales plan:

  • Price increases alone won't carry growth. Guests are watching value closely, so the next gains have to come from traffic, frequency and a better mix.
  • Online visibility is the new front door. Your Google profile, your reviews and your website shape the decision before a guest ever sees your menu.
  • Regulars are your most reliable revenue. Loyalty, personalized marketing and service recovery protect the guests you already have.

1. Optimize your Google Business Profile

Google Business Profile (GBP) is a free tool that controls how your restaurant appears in Google Search and Google Maps. When someone searches "Mexican restaurant near me", a complete, accurate profile is what puts you in the map results.

Completeness pays off directly. According to Google, customers are 2.7 times more likely to consider a business reputable, and 70% more likely to visit it, when they find a complete Business Profile on Search and Maps.

Here is how to optimize your Google Business Profile:

  • Fill in every field. Hours (including holiday hours), phone, website, menu link, ordering and reservation links, price range and attributes such as outdoor seating or vegetarian options.
  • Use your real-world name and the fewest categories that describe you. Google's guidelines ask you to represent your business as it appears on your signage, and to keep one profile per location.
  • Add fresh photos regularly. Show your dishes, your dining room and your team, and replace outdated menu photos when the menu changes.
  • Post updates and offers. Seasonal menus, events and limited-time items can be posted directly to your profile.
  • Keep details identical across locations and directories. Inconsistent addresses or hours confuse both guests and Google.

4. Set up a direct online ordering system

Third-party delivery apps bring reach, but they charge for it. DoorDash, for example, lists marketplace delivery commissions of 15%, 25% or 30% of each order depending on the plan. On thin margins, that commission can be larger than your profit on the order.

A direct online ordering system lets guests order from your own website or app. You still pay card processing and, if you offer it, delivery costs, but you keep the commission and the guest relationship. See what that could mean for you:

Direct ordering also helps in three other ways:

  • You own the guest data. Delivery apps keep the customer relationship. Your own channel collects emails, phone numbers and order history you can use for marketing.
  • You control the experience. Custom upsells, loyalty rewards and branded packaging all reinforce why your restaurant is worth coming back to.
  • You can raise the average order. Your own checkout can suggest add-ons ("Add guacamole for $2?") in the way you choose.

Most POS systems, including Toast and Square, offer online ordering that sends orders straight to your kitchen. The same systems usually support QR code ordering and pay-at-table for dine-in guests, which makes it easier to show photos and add-ons than a paper menu does.

5. Make third-party delivery platforms work for you

You shouldn't abandon delivery apps, since many guests discover restaurants there. The goal is to use them for reach and move repeat guests to your own channels over time.

Here's how to make third-party platforms work in your favor:

  • Optimize your listing like you would Google. Use a high-quality cover photo, write a description with phrases people actually search for ("late night tacos in Austin"), and keep hours accurate.
  • Engineer a delivery menu. Feature dishes that travel well and earn a healthy margin after commission, and consider slightly higher delivery prices where the platform allows it.
  • Protect your ratings. Platforms rank well-rated restaurants higher, so check order accuracy and packaging the way you would check plates in the dining room.
  • Invite guests to order direct. A card in the bag with a first-order offer on your own website turns platform guests into your guests.

6. Redesign your menu for profit

Your menu is your most important sales tool. Menu engineering means looking at each item's popularity and profit margin together, then designing the menu to steer guests toward the items that earn the most.

Here's how to design yours to drive higher-value choices:

  • Know your margins. Calculate the food cost of every item and sort items into four groups: popular and profitable, popular but low-margin, profitable but unpopular, and neither.
  • Give your best items the best spots. Place high-margin dishes at the top of each section or in a highlighted box where the eye lands first.
  • Use descriptive language. "Slow-braised short rib with roasted garlic mash" tells guests what they get and why it's worth the price.
  • Keep choices manageable. Long menus slow decisions and increase waste. Trim items that are neither popular nor profitable.

7. Use smart menu pricing

How you present prices influences what guests spend. In a 2009 Cornell University study, diners who saw prices written as plain numbers ("20") spent about 8% more than diners whose menus showed a dollar sign ("$20") or prices in words. The study was small, run in one restaurant, but it is easy and free to test.

A few more pricing practices to consider:

  • Anchor each category with a premium option. One high-priced item makes the rest of the section feel more reasonable.
  • Avoid price columns. Lining prices up on the right invites guests to scan for the cheapest item. Place each price at the end of its description instead.
  • Price with your costs in mind. Review prices whenever ingredient costs move, and raise them in small steps on items where guests are less price-sensitive.

8. Train staff to upsell and cross-sell

Your front-of-house team is your highest-leverage sales force. The difference between "Can I get you anything else?" and "Our truffle fries go really well with that burger, would you like to add them?" is real revenue on every table.

Good upselling feels like a recommendation, so train for that:

  • Teach the menu by tasting it. Staff recommend what they know and like.
  • Give them two or three specific suggestions per shift. A drink pairing, a shareable starter and a dessert are easy wins.
  • Track average check by server. Share the results and coach the team, while keeping the focus on guest experience.

9. Create combo meals and bundle deals

Bundles shift the guest's thinking from "how much is this?" to "how much am I getting?". They raise the average check while making the order feel like better value.

  • Pair high-demand items with high-margin ones. Build the bundle around a dish guests already love, then add sides, drinks or desserts with strong margins.
  • Make the value obvious. Show the individual prices next to the bundle price: "Normally $42, all three for $34."
  • Design bundles for occasions. Date night packages, family meals and office lunch sets target a specific need, which makes them easier to market.

10. Introduce premium and limited-time menu items

Limited-time items give guests a reason to visit now and a reason to come back. Starbucks' Pumpkin Spice Latte is the classic example: a seasonal drink guests look forward to every fall because it isn't available year-round.

Here's how to apply the same principle:

  • Launch a seasonal menu every quarter. Tie it to the season, a holiday or a local event.
  • Add a premium tier. One or two high-end items, such as a dry-aged steak or a chef's tasting plate, lift the average check and how guests perceive your restaurant.
  • Make it visible. Feature limited-time items on table cards, your Google profile, social media and the top of your online ordering page.
  • Let the data decide. When a limited-time item sells exceptionally well, consider adding it permanently.

11. Build a loyalty and rewards program

Keeping a guest costs far less than winning a new one. A widely cited Harvard Business Review article estimates that acquiring a new customer is 5 to 25 times more expensive than retaining an existing one. A restaurant loyalty program turns occasional visitors into regulars.

Loyalty also matters to guests: the National Restaurant Association reports that a solid majority of consumers say being a member of a loyalty program is important when they choose a restaurant.

  • Choose the right model. Points per dollar suit frequent, lower-ticket visits. Visit-based rewards ("your 10th meal is on us") work well for full-service dining.
  • Make it digital from day one. Run the program through your POS or ordering system so rewards connect to purchase history automatically.
  • Give a strong sign-up incentive. "Join and get a free appetizer on your next visit" converts browsers into members immediately.
  • Reward behavior you want. Bonus points on slow days or for trying new items move traffic where you need it.

12. Run targeted email and SMS campaigns

Social media reach is borrowed, but your email and SMS list is yours. Email remains one of the most cost-effective channels: in Litmus's State of Email research, most marketing leaders reported a return of $10 to $50 for every dollar spent on email.

Restaurants can make the most of these channels by keeping messages short, timely and useful:

  • Collect contacts at every touchpoint. Online orders, Wi-Fi sign-ins, reservations, loyalty sign-ups and feedback surveys.
  • Send messages tied to moments. A new seasonal menu, a rainy-day delivery offer, or a reminder before a holiday.
  • Respect frequency. A few relevant messages a month perform better than daily blasts that lead to unsubscribes.

13. Send personalized offers based on guest data

Generic discounts are easy to ignore. A message that says "Our new spicy tuna roll just dropped, we thought you'd want to know" to a guest who orders sushi every Friday feels like service.

Personalization starts with first-party guest data: what each guest orders, when they visit and what they say in feedback. Use it to build a few simple segments:

  • Lapsed guests: regulars who haven't visited in 60 days get a "we miss you" offer.
  • Favorite items: guests who order a dish often hear first when it changes or returns.
  • Occasions: birthdays and anniversaries get a small reward that brings a whole table.

14. Launch a catering and group ordering program

A single catering order can equal many individual covers, often with less pressure on your front of house. Chick-fil-A is a good example of a chain that made catering a standard part of its offer.

Here's how to build a catering program for your restaurant:

  • Build a short catering menu. Take 8 to 12 of your best-selling dishes that travel well and hold temperature.
  • Set clear logistics upfront. Minimum order size, notice required, delivery radius and serving equipment, all on a dedicated catering page.
  • Go after nearby offices first. A one-page catering menu with a first-order offer is enough to open the door.

15. Use social media and local creators

A social media presence with personality keeps your restaurant in people's minds, and it doesn't need a big budget. Authentic, behind-the-scenes content often performs best: a chef pulling fresh pasta, the team setting up for service, a signature dish coming off the grill.

Chipotle built a large TikTok following with exactly this mix of humor, employee culture and food prep videos.

You can also partner with local food creators. Smaller accounts with an engaged local audience are often a better fit for a restaurant than large influencers:

  • Search for local food creators. Look up hashtags like #[yourcity]eats or #[yourcity]foodie on Instagram and TikTok.
  • Make the ask simple. Invite them for a complimentary tasting, with no strings attached.
  • Track what drives results. Give each creator a unique code or link so you can see which posts bring orders or reservations.

16. Host community events and experiential dining nights

Events give guests a reason to visit on a specific date, and a story to tell afterwards. Dinner in the Sky takes this to the extreme by suspending a dining table high in the air. You don't need to go that far, but the principle is the same.

  • Partner with local businesses. A brewery for a beer pairing night or a local artist for a gallery dinner. Cross-promotion doubles the audience.
  • Fill slow nights. Trivia, tasting menus and chef's tables turn a quiet Tuesday into a busy one.
  • Sell tickets for high-demand nights. Ticketing secures revenue upfront and reduces no-shows.

17. Recover unhappy guests and keep quality consistent

Every unhappy guest is a sales risk: they may not come back, and they may tell others. Fast service recovery turns many of them around.

Across six months of POS-linked survey data from a quick-service brand with 100+ locations, Momos saw a clear pattern: guests who received a recovery offer and redeemed it came back more often than guests who had a good experience in the first place.

  • Ask every guest for feedback. A short survey after each order catches problems that never become public reviews.
  • Respond within hours. Apologize, fix the issue and offer a reason to return. Momos Customer Recovery automates this across locations.
  • Fix the root cause. Standard operating procedures for recipes, plating and opening checklists keep every service consistent, so the same complaint doesn't repeat.

How To Measure Restaurant Sales Growth

Track a few numbers every week so you know which strategies work:

  • Same-store sales: sales compared with the same period last year, for locations open in both periods. It removes the effect of new openings.
  • Guest count (traffic): the number of checks or covers. Rising sales with falling traffic usually means price increases are doing the work.
  • Average check: sales divided by checks or covers. It shows the effect of upselling, bundles and menu changes.
  • Visit frequency and repeat guest rate: how often guests return, from your loyalty program or CRM.
  • Channel mix: the share of sales from dine-in, direct online ordering and delivery apps, and the margin of each.
  • Ratings and review volume: your average rating, recent review count and review response rate for each location.

Which Strategies To Start With

You don't need all 17 at once. A practical order for most restaurants:

  1. This week: complete your Google Business Profile, start replying to every review, and remove the dollar signs from your menu.
  2. This month: launch direct online ordering, brief your team on two or three upsells, and create one bundle.
  3. This quarter: start a digital loyalty program, collect guest contacts, and run your first email or SMS campaign.
  4. Ongoing: measure the numbers above, recover unhappy guests quickly, and refresh your menu with limited-time items.

How To Increase Restaurant Sales: FAQ

What is the fastest way to increase restaurant sales?

Raising the average check is usually the fastest, because it works with the guests you already have. Staff upselling, bundles and better menu placement can show results within weeks. Completing your Google Business Profile and replying to reviews are also quick wins that bring in new guests.

How can I increase restaurant sales without discounting?

Focus on value instead of price: menu engineering, premium and limited-time items, bundles, better reviews and loyalty rewards. Discounts work best when they're targeted, such as a win-back offer to lapsed guests, instead of applied to everyone.

How do online reviews affect restaurant sales?

Reviews influence whether guests consider you at all. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, and research from Harvard Business School linked a one-star rating increase to a 5% to 9% revenue increase for independent restaurants.

How do I increase restaurant sales on slow days?

Give guests a specific reason to come: a themed night, a limited-time menu, bonus loyalty points, or a targeted SMS offer to guests who live nearby. Track which offers fill seats and repeat them.

How do I know which strategy is working?

Measure guest count, average check and visit frequency separately, and change one thing at a time where you can. If sales rise because the average check grew, your upselling and menu changes are working; if guest count grew, your visibility and reputation efforts are.
